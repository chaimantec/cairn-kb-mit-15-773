# Decoding and sampling

A [large language model](large-language-models.md) generates text one token at a time. At each step its
last softmax gives a probability for every token in the vocabulary, and one token must be chosen. "The
process of choosing a token from the probability distribution generated by the softmax is called
**decoding**" ([lecture 9](09-large-language-models.md), slide 43; ≈31:11). Getting really good
performance out of systems like ChatGPT means being "quite thoughtful about how to decode". The softmax
table itself is fixed for a given input: "You literally get the table, and then you literally can forget
how you got the table. And now decoding starts" (≈35:47).

The running example is the prompt "It was a dark and ____", where the slides' table gives "stormy" 0.6,
"rainy" 0.3, "aardvark" 0.0003 and "zebra" 0.00009 among the roughly 50,000 entries (slides 32 and 44).

## Two simple options

**Greedy decoding** chooses the highest-probability token, here "stormy" (slide 44; ≈31:56). Slide 44's
footnote adds that it is "Not necessarily optimal. Beam Search can help", which the lecture does not
take further.

**Random sampling** draws a token in proportion to its probability. Sampled 100 times from the table,
"60 times, you probably get stormy", and now and then something strange like "aardvark" (≈31:56–32:42).

Which to use depends on the task (slide 45; ≈32:42–35:02):

| Greedy decoding | Random sampling |
| --- | --- |
| Factual accuracy of the response is important | Diversity and "creativity" of the responses are important |
| Deterministic outputs are needed | Stochastic outputs are OK |

For a given input the table never changes, so greedy decoding always gives the same answer. That suits
a customer-support agent, and for "math questions, reasoning questions, logic questions", the lecturer
keeps it "as greedy as possible". Random sampling suits poems, haikus and screenplays, and gives a chatbot
variety so it does not sound "robotic". It is also why ChatGPT can give a different answer to the same
prompt (≈35:47).

## The trouble with random sampling: the head and the tail

Sorted from high to low, the softmax's distribution should have a **short head** of good tokens and a
**long tail** of irrelevant ones, and we want sampling to pick from the head (slide 46; ≈36:33–39:44).
But "while the probability of choosing any individual token in the tail is small, the probability of
sampling *some* token from the tail can still be high". With 0.9 on the top two words there is still
"a 10% probability it's going to be one of these words" (≈39:44). And the model cannot recover from a bad
word, because it becomes part of the input: "it'll just go off the rails" (≈39:44–40:30). There is no
technical definition of where the head ends. It varies by question and by model (≈40:30).

The lecture's example comes from GPT-3.5 (slides 47–50; ≈40:30–43:40). For "Students at the MIT Sloan
School of Management are", the top five next words are "invited" 3.18%, "given" 2.69%, "expected" 2.59%,
"required" 2.50% and "able" 2.09%. "Invited" leads to "… to submit their original white papers to the
annual MIT", which is reasonable. The tail words "masters" (0.05%) and "spending" (0.11%) lead to "…
masters of chaos. They routinely blow past deadlines, fracture" and "… spending the semester learning life
skills — through knitting socks".

## Tuning random sampling

Three methods **first modify the distribution, then sample** from it (slides 51–55), each emphasizing the
head over the tail.

### Top-K sampling

"Consider only the *K* most probable words. Renormalize their probabilities so they add up to 1. Do
random sampling from this set" (slide 51; ≈46:44–48:17). With $K = 2$ only "stormy" (0.6) and "rainy"
(0.2) remain; renormalized, each divided by their total of 0.8, they become 0.75 and 0.25 (slide 52).
Top-K is very commonly used, but it assumes the head has the same number of words whatever the input,
"not a well-supported assumption". Useful values of $K$, such as 10 to 50, are problem dependent.

### Top-P (nucleus) sampling

"Consider the minimum set of words whose total probability exceeds *P*. Renormalize their probabilities so
they add up to 1. Do random sampling from this set" (slide 53; ≈48:17–49:03). The number of words now
varies with the input: sometimes two, sometimes 20. With $P = 0.9$, the probabilities 0.6, 0.2 and 0.1 reach
0.9 at "foggy", so three words are kept, renormalized to 0.67, 0.22 and 0.11 (slide 54), each divided by
0.9. The lecturer finds it "even more effective" than top-K, because "it doesn't hardcode the number of
words". The set keeps growing until its total crosses $P$ (≈49:03). A fixed probability cutoff per word
has the same trouble in another form, since you cannot know how many words will cross it (≈49:50–50:35).
Top-K and top-P can be used together: stop at $K$ words or at total $P$, whichever comes first (≈50:35).

### Temperature

"By adjusting this parameter, we can make the 'head' of the distribution less or more important" (slide
55). Where top-K and top-P draw a hard line, temperature is "a softer way to do the same thing"
(≈51:21). The lecturer derives it on an iPad, not in the deck, so this follows the narration
(≈51:21–56:01).

Let $a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n$ be the numbers in the output layer, one per word in the vocabulary. The
[softmax](multiclass-classification.md) gives word $i$ the probability $e^{a_i}$ divided by the sum of all
the $e^{a}$'s. Temperature $T$ divides every $a$ first:

$$\text{probability of word } i = \frac{e^{a_i/T}}{e^{a_1/T} + e^{a_2/T} + \dots + e^{a_n/T}}$$

With $T = 1$ this is the ordinary softmax. As $T$ approaches 0, the biggest $a$ becomes "massive" and its
word's probability goes to 1, with every other word's going to 0, so "stormy" might get 0.999. Sampling
from such a distribution always takes the top word: **temperature 0 is greedy decoding** (≈53:43–56:01).
As $T$ grows, the distribution flattens until every word is about equally likely and sampling is "truly
random" (≈56:01).

## In practice: the OpenAI Playground

In the OpenAI Playground (lecture 9, slide 57; ≈56:50–1:00:45) temperature defaults to 1 and can be set
to 0, for greedy decoding, or above 1. Top-P can be set; top-K cannot. The Playground can also show the
probability of each generated token. With the temperature at 0, the MIT Sloan prompt gives "invited" every
time. Set to its maximum of 2, it produces garbage: "Ladies and gentlemen, I present to you a modern large
language model". People use these controls heavily for debugging, and "when you're building applications
on top of these large language models, you've got to be very, very careful with these parameters". Bing's
"creative, balanced, precise" settings, the lecturer says, adjust them under the hood (≈36:33).
