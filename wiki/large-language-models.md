# Large language models

A **large language model** (LLM) such as GPT-3 is, in this course's construction, a stack of
transformer blocks trained on a vast amount of text to do one thing: **predict the next word**. Text is
generated by predicting a word, appending it to the input and predicting again. The course builds this
in [lecture 9](09-large-language-models.md), the first of two lectures on LLMs, which its roadmap calls
"Large Language Models (1/2)" (lecture 9, slide 1). The second, [lecture 10](10-adapting-llms.md), turns the
base model into an assistant by [instruction tuning](instruction-tuning.md), and adapts it to a task by
prompting, [retrieval-augmented generation](retrieval-augmented-generation.md) or fine-tuning with LoRA
([adapting LLMs](adapting-llms.md)).

## Next word prediction: a second self-supervised task

To pretrain on text, which has no labels, [lecture 8](08-transformers-and-self-supervised-learning.md)
**masked** words and trained a transformer encoder to fill in the blanks, which gave BERT (see
[self-supervised learning](self-supervised-learning.md)). **Next word prediction** is another
self-supervised task, "weirdly more interesting and powerful". In a sense it is a special case of
masking that always blanks the **last** word (lecture 9, slides 2–3; ≈1:32–2:20). Every prefix of a
sentence becomes an input and the word after it the label: "The" → "mission", "The mission" → "of", and
so on. Slide 3's footnote calls it "Causal Language Modeling", against masking's "Masked Language
Modeling" (slide 2).

One sentence supplies all these examples at once. Stack it against a copy of itself shifted by one word,
so the input is "the cat sat on the" and the output is "cat sat on the mat" (slide 5; ≈3:10–4:44). The
model is BERT's: positional input embeddings, a transformer stack, contextual embeddings, dense ReLU
layers, and a softmax at **every position** whose categories are the whole vocabulary, about 50,000
words (slides 6–10; ≈4:44–6:16). Lecture 10 opens by correcting the dense layer: it has "linear
activations (not ReLU)" and feeds the softmax, and its output is as long as the vocabulary
([lecture 10](10-adapting-llms.md), slide 3; ≈0:47–1:32). The loss is the categorical cross-entropy at each position, averaged
over the positions (slide 11; ≈9:24–11:47; see [loss functions](loss-functions.md)).

## Why a plain encoder fails, and causal self-attention

In self-attention every word's contextual embedding is a weighted average over **all** the words in the
sentence (slide 13). Trained for next word prediction, the encoder would cheat: to predict "sat" it should
use only "the cat", but self-attention can see "sat" in the input and simply copy it (slides 14–16;
≈7:03–9:24).

The fix is **causal self-attention**, also called **masked self-attention**: "When calculating the
contextual embedding of a word, give zero weight to 'future' words" (slides 17–20; ≈11:47–14:05). In the
table of attention weights, one row per word, zero every cell above the diagonal and renormalize, so each
row still adds up to 1. The embedding of "sat" is then a weighted average of "the", "cat" and "sat" only.
"Causal" has no connection to causality (≈14:52).

A transformer block whose attention heads are causal is a **Transformer Causal Encoder** (TCE). Because
the decoder of the original encoder-decoder transformer, built for machine translation, uses "Masked (aka
Causal) Multi-Head Attention", the causal encoder is also called the **Transformer Decoder** (slides
26–29; ≈14:52–17:12). The word "decoder" therefore has two meanings, and in this course, which skips
translation, it means the causal encoder. The bidirectional encoder serves masked prediction (BERT); the
causal one serves next word prediction (GPT) (≈17:12–20:19). No unmasking is needed at inference, since
the prediction that matters, the last, may use every word before it anyway (≈18:02–19:34). See
[transformers](transformers.md#causal-self-attention-and-the-decoder).

## Generating text

Give a trained model "It was a dark and". Only the **last** position's softmax matters: it is a
probability for every word in the vocabulary, in which "stormy" might be the most likely at 0.6 (slides
31–32; ≈20:19–21:52). Choose a word from that table, append it to the input, and run the model again. The
new table makes "night" most likely at 0.95, so append that, and so on (slides 33–36; ≈21:52–23:25). The
loop can be stopped after a set number of tokens, or when the model produces punctuation such as a period
(≈22:40). "The basic building block is next word prediction, feed it back to the input, next word
prediction, keep on doing it. … Then, suddenly, it's writing entire novels for you" (≈23:25). How the
word is chosen from the table is **decoding**: see [decoding and sampling](decoding-and-sampling.md).

Models that generate sequences this way are **autoregressive** or **causal language models** (slide
38). "Autoregressive" is borrowed from econometrics: each prediction is fed back as an input to the next
(≈25:45–26:32). With them the course has all three uses of a transformer: sequence classification and
labeling with BERT-like models, and now **sequence generation** (slide 37; see
[natural language processing](natural-language-processing.md)).

## GPT-3

"GPT-3 is an autoregressive LLM!" (lecture 9, slide 39; ≈28:05–29:38). It has 96 transformer layers with
96 heads in each multi-head attention layer, and it was trained for next word prediction on about 30
billion sentences from the Internet and books. Slide 39's table, from the GPT-3 paper, lists the training
sets in tokens: Common Crawl (filtered) 410 billion, WebText2 19 billion, Books1 12 billion, Books2 55
billion and Wikipedia 3 billion. The "large" in LLM means only that it was trained "on lots of data with
lots of parameters. … Nothing more momentous than that". GPT-3's architecture is known from its paper;
GPT-4's was never published (≈28:51). Lecture 8 also mentions GPT-3's 96 blocks, in answer to a question
about stacking ([lecture 8](08-transformers-and-self-supervised-learning.md), ≈2:21).

GPT and GPT-2 were trained the same way, and the difference "was literally one of scale, scale of
network and scale of data": 117 million parameters for GPT, 1.5 billion for GPT-2 and 175 billion for
GPT-3 ([lecture 10](10-adapting-llms.md), slide 4; ≈1:32–2:22). Yet "more became different". GPT-3 could
continue a prompt coherently for paragraphs, where GPT-2 meandered after a few sentences, and it could
learn a task from a clear instruction or a few examples in the prompt, **in-context learning**, "an
emergent capability" nobody anticipated (lecture 10, slides 5–6 and 74; ≈3:09, ≈34:52).

Since "code is just text", one model of this kind serves text generation, code generation and
documentation, summarization, question answering and chatbots (slide 40; ≈29:38). "This simple idea of
text in, text out is just so flexible."

## What an LLM's answer is, and is not

An LLM "is basically doing some very rough and approximate pattern matching from all the training data".
Its response is "probabilistically plausible", with "no guarantee of correctness": asked to list the
awards the lecturer had won, one produced a list in which none was true (lecture 9, ≈43:40–44:27). Each
word narrows what can follow, like "the capital of France is" against "the capital of". The lecturer
passes on a name for this, "subtractive sculpting": each word chips away at a block of marble (≈45:12–46:44).
Because every generated word becomes input, a bad choice cannot be undone, and a model that samples a
word from the tail of the distribution "will go off the rails" (≈39:44–42:52; see
[decoding and sampling](decoding-and-sampling.md)).

## From base model to assistant

A base LLM is an autocomplete. GPT-3 "was not good at following user instructions": asked to help write
a note to a neighbour, it continued with lines about resume introductions, because it "wasn't
*explicitly* trained to generate good answers to user instructions" ([lecture 10](10-adapting-llms.md),
slides 7–13; ≈3:55–5:28). **Instruction tuning** trains it to: supervised fine-tuning on human-written
answers, a Reward Model trained on human rankings, and reinforcement learning from human feedback. That
turned GPT-3 into GPT-3.5/InstructGPT, and the same recipe on conversations gave ChatGPT (slides 15–51;
see [instruction tuning](instruction-tuning.md)). Adapting a model further, to a business task, is the
subject of [adapting LLMs](adapting-llms.md).

## The context window, and hallucination

An LLM's prompt and output together cannot exceed a fixed number of tokens, the **context window** (lecture
10, slide 77; ≈36:26). A chatbot has no memory of its own: "the entire conversation is fed in every single
time", so a long conversation uses up the window (slide 78; ≈37:13). This limit is why
[retrieval-augmented generation](retrieval-augmented-generation.md) retrieves only the most relevant
passages rather than sending a whole database.

Asked about something after its training cutoff, a model may give "a very eloquent, confident, wrong
answer". This is called **hallucination**. In lecture 10's Colab, GPT-3.5-turbo, trained on data up to
September 2021, named the wrong women's curling champions of the 2022 Winter Olympics (≈45:44–46:29).
Telling it to say "sorry, I don't know" when unsure, or giving it the relevant text as context, fixed the
answer (≈47:14–48:46).

## Tokens, not words

The lecture speaks of words for simplicity, but an LLM's vocabulary is made of **tokens**. GPT uses
**byte pair encoding**, which builds a vocabulary of characters, frequent whole words and frequent word
fragments, about 50,000 tokens for GPT-2 and GPT-3 and about 100,000 for GPT-4. That is how it keeps case
and punctuation and can use a word made up on the spot (lecture 9, slides 58–66; ≈1:00:45–1:14:23). See
[text vectorization](text-vectorization.md#beyond-stie-byte-pair-encoding).
